1 · The problem, from first principles

Every loan request is a bet. We are building the thing that prices the bet.

Someone asks for KES 5,000. The lender has a few seconds to decide. Lend to someone who never pays and the money is gone. Refuse someone who would have paid and a customer walks away. Today partners decide with simple rules or gut feel. We want them to decide with data, and to be able to explain every decision.

How I'm thinking about it: five steps, in this order

Step 1 · The decisionLend or not, and how much?Start from the business decision, never from the algorithm.
Step 2 · The predictionWhat's the chance she won't repay?We predict a percentage, not a yes/no. A percentage lets the business choose how much risk it accepts, and set prices.
Step 3 · The ruleCompare that chance to the moneyIf the expected loss is bigger than the expected profit, say no or lend less.
Step 4 · The explanationGive the top reasonsFor the customer, the partner and the regulator. "Pay on time for two months and you qualify."
Step 5 · The loopWatch, learn, retrainTrack who actually repaid, check the model still works, update it.

The model only does step 2. Steps 1, 3, 4 and 5 are what turn a model into a product the business can trust.

The two mistakes cost different amounts

Lend to someone who doesn't payWe lose most of the KES 5,000. Say about KES 4,000 after some recovery.
expensive
Refuse someone who would have paidWe miss the fee. About KES 500 on a 10% fee.
cheaper, but adds up

Because one mistake costs eight times the other, we don't need to be sure someone will default before saying no. The break-even point is 500 ÷ (500 + 4,000) ≈ 11%. Above roughly 11% risk, the loan loses money on average.

We're not asking the model "will she default, yes or no?" We're asking "how risky is she?", then letting the money decide where the line goes.

What "good" looks like

  • More profit than the partner makes today (or than approving everyone).
  • Keep good customers: don't turn away people who would have paid.
  • Explain every decision in plain words.
  • Fast: an answer in well under a second while the customer waits.
  • Fair: similar people get similar treatment, checked for every group.
  • Keeps working: we notice when customers change and the model needs updating.
For engineers

Target: probability of default (PD) within a defined window, e.g. 30+ days past due on any of the first instalments. Decision rule: approve if (1 − PD)·M − PD·L > 0, i.e. PD < M/(M+L), where M = margin if repaid, L = loss given default × exposure. Model selection metric: expected profit on held-out loans, with AUC/KS for ranking, Brier/calibration for honest probabilities, and fairness and stability (PSI) as gates.

2 · Data and features

What the models learned from, and why each fact is there

A "feature" is one fact about a person that might help predict whether they repay. Choosing features is where most of the real thinking happens. A good model on bad features is still a bad model.

Where the practice data comes from

Home Credit is an international lender to people with little or no credit history, the same kind of customer as M-KOPA or Aventus. In 2018 it published anonymised loan data for a public competition. We downloaded a free public copy.

  • past loan applications, one per person, each marked "repaid" or "had trouble repaying". About had trouble.
  • About 13 million past instalments from their earlier loans: what was due, when, and what they actually paid. From this we learn each person's repayment habits.

Not Kenyan, not M-PESA, no names or phone numbers. Money amounts are in the dataset's own currency, not KES. It proves the method; the numbers must be re-earned on Safaricom data.

How we chose the 15 features

  1. Start from what a lender would ask: can they afford it, do they pay on time, are they stable, what do others say about them?
  2. Keep only facts Safaricom could supply (with consent), so the prototype carries straight over.
  3. Turn raw history into habits: 13M payment records become "how often late", "how late", "how often short".
  4. Leave out what's unfair or invasive: gender (kept only to check fairness), friends' defaults, home details that are mostly blank, document checkboxes.
  5. Check the model actually uses them, in a sensible direction: more late payments must never lower risk.

6 · Deploy and serve

Two lanes: a slow lane that builds the model, a fast lane that answers customers

Training happens in the background on a schedule. Serving happens every time someone taps "Borrow". Keeping them separate is what makes it fast, safe and easy to update.

Slow lane: build and approve a model weekly or monthly

Fast lane: answer one loan request every request, < 100 ms target

The partner never sees raw data. They get a risk, a decision, a limit, and reasons in plain words.

Rolling out safely

  1. Shadow (2–4 weeks). The model scores every request but decides nothing. We compare it with the partner's current decisions and outcomes.
  2. Small slice (5–10%). The model decides for a small share of customers. We compare repayment against the rest.
  3. Champion and challenger. The best model serves most traffic; a new candidate gets a small share and must win on profit to take over.
  4. Fallback. If the service is slow or down, a tiny points-card (logistic) model or the partner's existing rule answers. A loan request never hangs.
For engineers
  • Runtime: FastAPI service in its own repo → Jenkins builds one image (AD-2) → runs on Iguazio next to Doris as a stateless MLRun application (AD-3), same pattern as the Conversational Intelligence backend.
  • Features: computed daily by an MLRun job from Doris; pushed to Redis (already in the stack) for millisecond lookups. Request fields (amount, tenor) come live. One shared feature module for training and serving, so the two can't disagree.
  • Models: versioned in a registry (MLflow locally today, MLRun once access is approved), each with a model card. Cut-offs live in the partner's YAML, so a policy change is config, not code.
  • Scale: CPU only, no GPU. Model loaded in memory; add containers horizontally. Timeout 200 ms → fallback.
  • Audit: every request logged asynchronously with features, model version, score, decision and reasons, written to Doris.
  • Access: per-partner API keys, consent ID required, hashed customer references only; OpenMetadata catalogues the feature definitions.
7 · Evaluate and monitor

Tests before launch, health checks after

A model is like a new loan officer. Before hiring, we test them. After hiring, we review their decisions. Customers change, the economy changes, so the model has to be watched and retrained.

Before launch: the tests a model must pass

TestIn plain wordsRule to passEBM todayStatus
For engineers

Offline evaluation on a stratified 20% holdout today. On Safaricom data: out-of-time validation (train on older months, test on the most recent), plus a back-test across several cohorts. PSI = Σ (actual% − expected%) · ln(actual% / expected%) over 10 score bands; < 0.1 stable, 0.1–0.25 watch, > 0.25 act.

We don't need a new tool for this. The monitoring numbers get written to Doris and shown in Superset, the same way our dashboards work today.

When do we retrain?

  • Applicants have shifted (stability index above 0.25).
  • Performance on recent loans has dropped.
  • The partner launches a new product or changes policy.
  • Otherwise on a schedule, every quarter.

A retrained model is a challenger: it has to pass the tests above and beat the current champion before it serves anyone.

The blind spot every lender has

We only ever learn whether approved people repay. If the model wrongly rejects a group, we never find out, because they never get a loan. Over time the model can lock in its own mistakes.

The fix: give a small, controlled number of near-the-line applicants a starter loan anyway, and learn from how they do. It's a small, budgeted cost that keeps the model honest.

For engineers

This is the reject-inference / selection-bias problem. Mitigations: randomised exploration band near the cut-off, starter-limit policy for the grey zone, and reweighting or bureau-outcome proxies for declined applicants.
